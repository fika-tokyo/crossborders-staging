# A Shinjuku building with only a minpaku license — and the other half of its income, sealed away

**— An investment simulation for an existing Shinjuku building: same building, roughly double the yield**

CROSSBORDERS CO., LTD. / July 2026

We operate our own hotels in the Shinjuku-sanchome area, so we have a first-hand feel for this market's daily room rates and occupancy. When a whole building a few minutes' walk away came up for sale, we ran the numbers straight away.

It is an apartment building currently operated as minpaku (private lodging): reinforced-concrete, six storeys, 14 rooms, of which 13 are already registered under the Private Lodging Business Act. It sits in a commercial zone, four minutes' walk from Shinjuku-sanchome Station, with a certificate of final inspection.

The conclusion of the simulation, up front:

**In the same building, the revenue yield is about 5.86% as it stands today (minpaku, capped at 180 operating days a year). Convert it to a hotel business (ryokan-gyo) license with 365-day operation, and it is about 10.38%. Without adding a single room, the earning power roughly doubles.**

*Note: revenue yield = annual gross revenue ÷ approximate total investment (including acquisition costs). Operating and holding costs are not deducted, so this is calculated differently from the gross rental yield used for residential income property. Converting to a hotel business license requires separate costs for change of building use, fire-safety works and licensing. The three-scenario model and its assumptions are available in individual consultations.*

Why such a gap — and why now? Three things are happening at the same time, and each of them comes with data.

## Event one: supply — new hotels are locked out by construction costs

**Start with construction costs.** The cost per tsubo for reinforced-concrete construction in the Tokyo area (condominium standard) has moved as follows:

| **Year** | **Construction cost per tsubo** | **vs. 2012** |
|---|---|---|
| 2012 (trough) | ¥701,000 | — |
| 2023 | ¥1,184,000 | +69% |
| 2024 | ¥1,412,000 | +101% |
| 2025 | **¥1,618,000** | **+131%** |

(Source: archi-book, compiled from MLIT "Building Construction Starts Statistics." Indicative levels; actual costs vary by specification.)

Even taking 2015 rather than the 2012 trough as the baseline, the Construction Price Research Institute's construction cost index (Tokyo, RC apartment buildings) reached **143.3 in March 2026, up 5.5% year on year** — more than 40% higher over a decade. The civil-engineering materials price index has now risen year on year for **28 consecutive months** (source: Construction Research Institute, "Construction Cost Index"). Whichever year you start from, the curve has not turned back.

**And the problem is not just "expensive" — it is "unobtainable."** The situation in the Middle East has brought supply concerns and price rises for petroleum-based materials such as paints and thinners, asphalt, waterproofing and insulation (source: Construction Research Institute Middle East reports, Kensetsu Bukka monthly, May–June 2026). One telling example: **from April 13, 2026, TOTO suspended new orders for its entire range of unit bathrooms due to material shortages, with no restart date announced** — and unit bathrooms are among the most indispensable components in building or converting accommodation. The 13 rooms in this building already have their bathrooms installed.

The result of rising costs is already visible on the supply side. According to market research by JLL, the international property consultancy, **Japan's new hotel supply is only about 1.7% of existing stock — the lowest in Asia-Pacific** (the highest, Vietnam, is at 21.3%), with Tokyo, Osaka, Kyoto and Fukuoka all around 2%. JLL's head of advisory explains that construction cost inflation weighs heavily, and a significant increase in new openings over the next few years is hard to imagine (source: JLL market seminar, "Japan's hotel market and the outlook for 2026").

Put together: **over the next few years, existing accommodation assets will face less new competition than at any time in the past.**

## Event two: regulation — two moves in one month, in opposite directions

This is the part most worth attention, because it comes with specific dates and document numbers.

**On June 15**, the amended Hotel Business Act enforcement order took effect (source: MHLW, "Partial amendment to the Hotel Business Act Enforcement Order"). The bar for obtaining a hotel business license dropped sharply: the minimum room counts — previously 10 rooms for hotel operation, 5 for ryokan — were abolished, so **applications can now be made from a single room**; ICT-based identity checks were formally recognized, and staffless operation was written into the regulations. For owners of existing buildings, the hurdle to converting a property into a facility that can operate 365 days a year has never been lower.

**On July 15**, exactly one month later, the Japan Tourism Agency, MLIT and MHLW jointly issued a technical advisory to municipalities nationwide concerning "zero-day restrictions" on registered minpaku properties. The "zero-day rule" that the national government had long deemed "inappropriate" is now effectively tolerated — municipalities can restrict, even effectively prohibit, minpaku operation in designated areas by ordinance. The notice states explicitly that in areas where harm has already arisen, restrictions may be imposed **not only on new entrants but on existing minpaku operators as well** (source: JTA press release, July 15, 2026).

Now consider where Shinjuku stands. Shinjuku Ward has **3,506 registered minpaku properties — the most of Tokyo's 23 wards** — and the strictest enforcement: **in November 2025 the ward issued suspension orders to 16 facilities and closure orders to 4 operators in a single action** (source: Shinjuku Ward, "Publication of violators of the Private Lodging Business Act"). Judging by registration numbers and enforcement intensity, if any ward moves first to use the new zero-day tool, Shinjuku is likely to be near the top of the list.

Hotel licensing loosened; minpaku tightened — one month apart. It is hard to call that direction a coincidence.

For owners of minpaku properties, this means: **the certainty of a "minpaku license already obtained" is declining, while the hotel business license is currently the only operating format outside the reach of these ordinances.** This building is in a commercial zone, is not subject to Shinjuku Ward's weekday-operation restrictions under its supplementary ordinance, and faces no zoning obstacle to hotel conversion.

## Event three: demand — volume is correcting, prices are rising

On the demand side, we want to show the full picture, including the unfavourable parts.

**Volume: correcting, driven mainly by a single market.** Inbound visitors totalled 21.08 million in the first half of 2026, down 2.0% year on year — the first negative first half in five years. June alone was down 6.8%, driven overwhelmingly by the China market (June: **−57.3%** year on year). Yet in the same June, South Korea sent 787,000 visitors (+7.8%), Taiwan 670,000 (+14.6%), the US 355,000 (+2.7%) and Hong Kong 214,000 (+28.5%) — **fifteen markets set all-time records for June** (source: JNTO visitor arrivals, June 2026 estimates).

**Price: rising.** Tokyo's average hotel ADR in March 2026 was **¥36,700, up 11.1% year on year** (source: Metro Engines Research / JTA "Overnight Travel Statistics Survey"). In dollar terms Tokyo's ADR is about $188.5 — still clearly below Singapore ($238.1), London ($249.9), New York ($305.9) and Paris ($373.7) (source: JLL, at ¥155 to the dollar).

Falling volume and rising prices — why is that not a contradiction? **Precisely because new supply is locked out by construction costs (event one), ADR can keep climbing even while visitor numbers correct. "Lower volume, higher price" is not a temporary quirk; it is the structural result of supply contraction.**

A market like this punishes overpriced assets, and relatively favours assets with headroom in room rates and no dependence on a single source market. This building's operating plan is set accordingly: **it does not assume a recovery in Chinese demand**, targeting instead South Korea, Taiwan, Europe/North America and Southeast Asia.

## So: this building's numbers

Supply contraction, a regulatory turn, and demand splitting between volume and price — the conclusion of all three lands in this table (the denominator in each case is approximate total investment including acquisition costs):

| **Operating format** | **Annual gross revenue** | **Revenue yield** |
|---|---|---|
| Residential letting (full occupancy) | c. ¥23.5M | c. 3.4% |
| Minpaku, 180-day cap (as is) | c. ¥43.8M | c. **5.86%** |
| Hotel license, 365-day operation | c. ¥80.96M | c. **10.38%** |

Between these three rows, the location does not change, the room count does not change, and demand does not change. Only one thing differs: the type of operating license. The minpaku law caps operation at 180 days a year — for more than half the year, these 14 fitted-out, guest-ready rooms must legally sit empty. Switch the minpaku registration to a hotel business (simple lodging) license, and that cap disappears.

**The obvious question: why doesn't the seller obtain the license and then sell?** Because licensing and 365-day operation form a chain of work that requires specialist capability — compliance surveys under the Hotel Business Act, Fire Service Act and Building Standards Act; prior consultations with the public health centre and fire department; change of use and license application; then revenue management and multilingual operation after opening. None of this is the seller's core business, so the building is priced for sale as minpaku, as is. In other words, **the income gap between the two formats is not a market oversight — it is the price of that chain of capability itself.** That chain happens to be our business: we operate our own hotel in the same trading area, and every operating assumption in this article can be checked against our own property's actual data.

Look also at how the valuation breaks down. Working back from replacement cost, about 84% of this building's total price corresponds to the commercial land in Shinjuku 5-chome; the building accounts for only about 16%. **Rebuilding that 16% (about ¥112M) from scratch today would cost about ¥200M in construction alone (versus about ¥90M in 2012) — and with core components such as unit bathrooms supply-constrained, even the construction schedule would be hard to pin down.**

Our assumptions are not aggressive. Public information on nearby competitors suggests this trading area averages around ADR ¥16,940 at 80% occupancy; a 22㎡ minpaku unit in the same block, four minutes' walk away, actually operates at around ADR ¥19,000 and 75% occupancy (source: OTA public listings / our own research). For reference, the average ADR of Tokyo business hotels is ¥21,400 (source: Metro Engines Research, April 2026, N=895 properties). **Even in a conservative scenario set below the competitor average, 365-day operation earns about 1.6 times the current minpaku income** — the value of hotel conversion holds in conservative, base and upside scenarios alike; only the size of the gap changes.
